The Basics of Social Network Analysis

Node Neighborhoods

Node Neighborhoods

  • Neighborhood (\(\mathcal{N}(v)\)):
    • The set of nodes that are directly connected (adjacent) to node \(v\).
    • These are a node’s immediate neighbors.
    • Isolates have an empty neighborhood set (\(\emptyset\)).

Node Neighborhoods: Example

  • Let’s look at the graph on the right:
    • Focal Node: 4 (in tomato)
    • Neighborhood: \(\mathcal{N}(4) = \{3, 7\}\) (in gold)
  • Another example:
    • \(\mathcal{N}(1) = \{3\}\)

Neighborhood Intersection

  • Neighborhood Intersection (\(\mathcal{N}(u) \cap \mathcal{N}(v)\)) is the set of common neighbors connected to both \(u\) and \(v\).
  • In our graph:
    • \(N(1) = \{3\}\)
    • \(N(4) = \{3, 7\}\)
  • The intersection is the single node they share:
    • \(N(1) \cap N(4) = \{3\}\)

Neighborhood Intersection: Example 2

  • Let’s find the neighborhood intersection of nodes 5 and 3.
  • First, list their individual neighborhood sets:
    • \(\mathcal{N}(5) = \{2, 6\}\)
    • \(\mathcal{N}(3) = \{1, 2, 4\}\)
  • The intersection is the set of common neighbors they share:
    • \(\mathcal{N}(5) \cap \mathcal{N}(3) = \{2\}\)

Neighborhood Intersection: Example 2 (Graph)

  • Nodes 5 and 3 (in tomato) are not directly connected.
  • However, they share a common neighbor: node 2 (in gold).
  • Therefore, their neighborhood intersection is: \[\mathcal{N}(5) \cap \mathcal{N}(3) = \{2\}\]

Neighborhood Union

  • Neighborhood Union (\(\mathcal{N}(u) \cup \mathcal{N}(v)\)) is the set of all nodes connected to either \(u\) or \(v\).
  • In our graph:
    • \(N(1) = \{3\}\)
    • \(N(4) = \{3, 7\}\)
  • The union contains all unique neighbors from both sets:
    • \(N(1) \cup N(4) = \{3, 7\}\)

Neighborhood Union: Example 2

  • Let’s find the neighborhood union of nodes 5 and 3.
  • First, list their individual neighborhood sets:
    • \(\mathcal{N}(5) = \{2, 6\}\)
    • \(\mathcal{N}(3) = \{1, 2, 4\}\)
  • The union combines all unique neighbors from both sets:
    • \(\mathcal{N}(5) \cup \mathcal{N}(3) = \{1, 2, 4, 6\}\)

Neighborhood Union: Example 2 (Graph)

  • Focal nodes 5 and 3 are shown in tomato.
  • The union of their neighbors (nodes 1, 2, 4, 6) is shown in gold.
  • Therefore, the union of neighbors is: \[\mathcal{N}(5) \cup \mathcal{N}(3) = \{1, 2, 4, 6\}\]

Directed Graph Elements & Metrics

Asymmetric Ties & Directed Graphs

  • Some relationships have an inherent directionality (asymmetric ties):
    • e.g., “A helps B”, “A seeks advice from B”.
  • Unlike symmetric ties, mutuality or reciprocity is not guaranteed by definition.
  • Reciprocity is an empirical event in the world (mutuality must be measured rather than assumed).

Anti-Symmetric Ties & Hierarchies

  • Some directional relationships strictly forbid reciprocity (anti-symmetric ties):
    • If \(A \rightarrow B\) exists, then \(B \rightarrow A\) is structurally or logically impossible (\(AB \implies \neg BA\)).
    • e.g., “A is the father of B”, “A is the supervisor of B”.
  • Unlike asymmetric ties, reciprocity is not optional—it is strictly prohibited.
  • Concatenating these relations across multiple nodes naturally generates directed trees or strict hierarchies with no cycles.

Directed Graph Representation

  • Asymmetric networks are represented as a directed graph (or digraph):
    • Simple lines are replaced with arrows indicating directionality.
    • Every edge has a source node (sender) and a destination node (receiver).
    • Unlike undirected graphs, node order matters: edge \(AB\) is distinct from \(BA\).

Directed Graph: An Example

  • Let’s look at the digraph on the right:
    • Arrows indicate the direction of the relationship.
    • Some ties are reciprocated (e.g., \(A \leftrightarrow B\) and \(B \leftrightarrow D\)).
    • Others are strictly asymmetric (e.g., \(E \rightarrow C\), \(G \rightarrow F\)).

Neighborhoods in Directed Graphs (1)

  • Because links are directional, nodes have two neighborhoods.
  • In-Neighborhood (\(N^{in}(v)\)):
    • Set of source nodes pointing to \(v\).
    • Example: \(N^{in}(D) = \{B, E, G\}\) (highlighted in gold, pointing to focal node D in tomato).

Neighborhoods in Directed Graphs (2)

  • Out-Neighborhood (\(N^{out}(v)\)):
    • Set of destination nodes that \(v\) points to.
    • Example: \(N^{out}(B) = \{A, C, D\}\) (highlighted in gold, pointing from focal node B in tomato).

Directed Neighborhoods: Reciprocity

  • A node appears in both neighborhoods only if there is a reciprocal (mutual) tie.
  • Example for Node B (in tomato):
    • In-Neighborhood: \(N^{in}(B) = \{A\}\)
    • Out-Neighborhood: \(N^{out}(B) = \{A, C, D\}\)

Directed Neighborhoods: Reciprocity Overlap

  • Since there is a reciprocal tie \(A \leftrightarrow B\), node A (in gold) belongs to both sets.
  • Therefore, the overlap is the intersection of these two neighborhoods: \[N^{in}(B) \cap N^{out}(B) = \{A\}\]

Directed Neighborhood Intersection

  • In directed graphs, neighborhood intersections can be defined for incoming or outgoing ties:
  • In-Neighborhood Intersection: Common source nodes pointing to both \(u\) and \(v\):
    • \(N^{in}(D) \cap N^{in}(A) = \{B\}\) (Node \(B\) points to both \(D\) and \(A\))
  • Out-Neighborhood Intersection: Common destination nodes that both \(u\) and \(v\) point to:
    • \(N^{out}(B) \cap N^{out}(E) = \{D\}\) (Both \(B\) and \(E\) point to \(D\))

Directed Neighborhood Intersection: Example

  • Let’s visualize the out-neighborhood intersection of nodes B and E (in tomato):
    • \(N^{out}(B) = \{A, C, D\}\)
    • \(N^{out}(E) = \{D\}\)
  • They share one common destination node:
    • \(N^{out}(B) \cap N^{out}(E) = \{D\}\) (in gold)

Directed Neighborhood Union

  • Similarly, directed neighborhood unions combine the sets of neighbors:
  • In-Neighborhood Union: Nodes pointing to either \(u\) or \(v\):
    • \(N^{in}(D) \cup N^{in}(A) = \{B, E, G\}\)
  • Out-Neighborhood Union: Nodes that either \(u\) or \(v\) points to:
    • \(N^{out}(B) \cup N^{out}(E) = \{A, C, D\}\)

Directed Neighborhood Union: Example

  • Let’s visualize the out-neighborhood union of nodes B and E (in tomato):
    • \(N^{out}(B) = \{A, C, D\}\)
    • \(N^{out}(E) = \{D\}\)
  • Combining all unique destination nodes gives:
    • \(N^{out}(B) \cup N^{out}(E) = \{A, C, D\}\) (in gold)

Special Classes of Directed Graphs

Symmetric Directed Graphs

  • Symmetric Directed Graph:
    • A digraph where for every directed arc \(u \rightarrow v\), the reverse arc \(v \rightarrow u\) is also present.
    • Formally: \(AB \iff BA\).
  • Key Properties:
    • It is a directed representation of an undirected (symmetric) graph.
    • Every connected dyad is reciprocal (Mutual).

Functional Graphs

  • Functional Graph:
    • A directed graph where every node has an outdegree of exactly 1: \[k^{out}_v = 1 \quad \text{for all } v \in V\]
  • Key Properties:
    • Represents a single-valued function \(f: V \rightarrow V\), mapping each node to exactly one target.
    • e.g., “Who is your best friend?” or “Who is your immediate boss?”

Oriented Graphs

  • Oriented Graph:
    • A directed graph obtained by choosing a single direction for each edge in an undirected graph.
  • Key Properties:
    • No reciprocal (mutual) arcs can exist: if \(u \rightarrow v\) is present, then \(v \rightarrow u\) is forbidden.
    • Represents purely non-reciprocal systems of interaction or asymmetric flows.

Directed Tree Graphs

  • Directed Tree Graph:
    • A directed, acyclic graph where a single root node has an indegree of 0, and all other nodes have an indegree of exactly 1: \[k^{in}_{root} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad k^{in}_v = 1 \quad \text{for all other } v\]
  • Key Properties:
    • Naturally models strict authority hierarchies or family trees.
    • Every node has exactly one parent or supervisor, with zero loops or overlapping chains of command.

Node Degree

Node Degree in Undirected Graphs

  • In an undirected graph, a node’s degree (\(k_i\)) has two equivalent definitions:
    1. Neighborhood Cardinality: The number of neighbors of node \(i\) (\(k_i = |\mathcal{N}(i)|\)).
    2. Incident Edges Count: The number of edges connected to node \(i\).
  • Sociological meaning: Degree Centrality (reflects popularity, influence, or activity).

Node Degree: An Example

  • Let’s calculate the degree for Node A (in tomato) on the right:
    • Neighbors of A: \(\mathcal{N}(A) = \{B, C, D, F\}\)
    • Neighborhood size: \(k_A = |\mathcal{N}(A)| = 4\)
    • Incident edges: \(AB\), \(AC\), \(AD\), and \(AF\) (\(k_A = 4\))
  • Thus, the degree of Node A is 4.

Endpoints in Undirected Graphs

  • Endpoints (or leaves/periphery):
    • Nodes that have a degree equal to one (\(k_i = 1\)).
    • They have just a single neighbor in the graph.

Endpoints: An Example

  • Let’s identify the endpoints in the graph on the right:
    • Nodes C and F (in tomato) are connected only to node A.
    • Since \(k_C = 1\) and \(k_F = 1\), they have degree one.
    • Therefore, C and F are the endpoints of this graph.

Node Degree in Directed Graphs: Indegree

  • Directed graphs have two distinct degrees for each node.
  • Indegree (\(k^{in}_i\)):
    • Number of edges pointing to node \(i\) (cardinality of \(N^{in}(i)\)).
    • Sociological meaning: Popularity, Prestige, or Status.

Indegree: An Example

  • Let’s find the indegree for Node D (in tomato) on the right:
    • Source nodes pointing to D: \(N^{in}(D) = \{B, E, G\}\) (in gold)
    • Indegree: \(k^{in}_D = |N^{in}(D)| = 3\)
  • Node D has 3 incoming arrows, representing a node with relatively high popularity or status.

Node Degree in Directed Graphs: Outdegree

  • Outdegree (\(k^{out}_i\)):
    • Number of edges pointing from node \(i\) (cardinality of \(N^{out}(i)\)).
    • Sociological meaning: Sociability or Activity.

Outdegree: An Example

  • Let’s find the outdegree for Node B (in tomato) on the right:
    • Target nodes that B points to: \(N^{out}(B) = \{A, C, D\}\) (in gold)
    • Outdegree: \(k^{out}_B = |N^{out}(B)| = 3\)
  • Node B has 3 outgoing arrows, representing a node with relatively high sociability or activity.

Types of Nodes in Directed Graphs

  • In a directed graph, we can distinguish three types of (non-isolate) nodes based on incoming and outgoing ties:
    1. Receivers: Nodes that receive ties but do not send them (\(k_{in} > 0\) and \(k_{out} = 0\)).
    2. Transmitters: Nodes that send ties but do not receive them (\(k_{in} = 0\) and \(k_{out} > 0\)).
    3. Carriers: Nodes that both receive and send ties (\(k_{in} > 0\) and \(k_{out} > 0\)).

Node Types: An Example

  • Looking at the directed graph on the right:
    • Receiver: Node C (only receives ties).
    • Transmitters: Nodes E and G (only send ties).
    • Carriers: Nodes A, B, D, F (both receive and send ties).

Basic Graph Metrics

Graph Order and Size

  • Graph Order (\(n\)):
    • The number of nodes in the graph (\(n = |V|\)).
    • Cardinality of the vertex set.
  • Graph Size (\(m\)):
    • The number of edges in the graph (\(m = |E|\)).
    • Cardinality of the edge set.

Order and Size: Undirected Example

  • Let’s look at the undirected graph on the right:
    • Node Set (\(V\)): \(\{A, B, C, D, E, F, G\}\)
    • Edge Set (\(E\)): \(\{AC, CD, DG,\)
      \(BE, EF, FB, CB\}\)
    • Graph Order (\(n = |V|\)): 7 nodes
    • Graph Size (\(m = |E|\)): 7 edges

Order and Size: Directed Example

  • Let’s look at the directed graph on the right:
    • Node Set (\(V\)): \(\{A, B, C, D, E, G\}\)
    • Edge Set (\(E\)): \(\{BA, BC, BD, ED,\)
      \(GD, AE, CG, AB\}\)
    • Graph Order (\(n = |V|\)): 6 nodes
    • Graph Size (\(m = |E|\)): 8 edges

Graph Maximum Size

  • The maximum size of a simple graph of order \(n\) is the number of edges in a complete graph (where every node is connected to every other node).
  • Undirected: \(Max(E) = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}\)
  • Directed: \(Max(E) = n(n - 1)\)

Maximum Size: Undirected Example

  • Let’s look at the undirected graph on the right:
    • Order (\(n\)): 7 nodes
  • Formula: \[Max(E) = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}\]

Maximum Size: Undirected Calculation

  • Order (\(n\)): 7 nodes (shown on the right)
  • Maximum possible size calculation: \[Max(E) = \frac{7 \times (7 - 1)}{2} = \frac{42}{2} = 21\text{ edges}\]
  • Interpretation:
    • A complete undirected graph with 7 nodes would have exactly 21 edges.

Maximum Size: Directed Example

  • Let’s look at the directed graph on the right:
    • Order (\(n\)): 6 nodes
  • Formula: \[Max(E) = n(n - 1)\]

Maximum Size: Directed Calculation

  • Order (\(n\)): 6 nodes (shown on the right)
  • Maximum possible size calculation: \[Max(E) = 6 \times (6 - 1) = 6 \times 5 = 30\text{ edges}\]
  • Interpretation:
    • A complete directed graph with 6 nodes would have exactly 30 directed arcs.

Graph Density: Formulas

  • Density (\(d\)) is the ratio of the observed number of edges (\(m\)) to the maximum possible number of edges.
  • It is a value between 0 (completely empty) and 1 (complete graph).
  • Undirected Graph: \[d = \frac{2m}{n(n-1)}\]

Graph Density: Formulas

  • Density (\(d\)) is the ratio of the observed number of edges (\(m\)) to the maximum possible number of edges.

  • It is a value between 0 (completely empty) and 1 (complete graph).

  • Directed Graph: \[d = \frac{m}{n(n-1)}\]

Graph Density: Interpretation

  • Connection Probability:
    • Density is the probability that any two randomly chosen nodes are connected.
  • Network Resilience:
    • Denser networks are generally more resilient and less vulnerable to breakdowns when key nodes are removed.

  • Graph Degree Set: A list of the degrees of every node in the graph.
  • Graph Degree Sequence: The degree set sorted in descending order.
  • This provides a “fingerprint” of the graph’s overall connectivity structure.

  • Degree Sequence (sorted degrees): \[(3, 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1)\]

  • In-Degrees: \(k^{in}_A=1, k^{in}_B=1, k^{in}_C=1, k^{in}_D=3, k^{in}_E=1, k^{in}_G=1\) (shown on the right)
  • In-Degree Set (unordered list of in-degrees): \[\{1, 1, 1, 3, 1, 1\}\]
  • In-Degree Sequence (sorted in-degrees): \[(3, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1)\]

Degree Range & Sum

  • Maximum Degree (\(k_{max}\)) and Minimum Degree (\(k_{min}\)).
  • Degree Range (\(k_r\)): \(k_{max} - k_{min}\).
  • Sum of Degrees (\(\sum k_i\)):
    • In an undirected graph, this is always twice the number of edges: \(\sum k_i = 2m\).

Degree Range & Sum: Example

  • Degrees: \(k_A=1, k_B=3, k_C=3, k_D=2, k_E=2, k_F=2, k_G=1\) (shown on the right)
  • Max & Min Degree:
    • \(k_{max} = 3\) (nodes B and C)
    • \(k_{min} = 1\) (nodes A and G)
  • Degree Range:
    • \(k_r = 3 - 1 = 2\)
  • Sum of Degrees:
    • \(\sum k_i = 1+3+3+2+2+2+1 = 14 = 2m\) (Matches!)

Graphical vs. Non-Graphical Sequences

  • A sequence of non-negative integers is graphical if there exists at least one simple, undirected graph (no loops, no multiple edges) that has it as its degree sequence.
  • Otherwise, the sequence is non-graphical—no such simple graph can possibly exist in the real world.
  • Why can’t certain sequences exist?
    • They violate core mathematical rules of graph structure!

Two Quick Tests for Graphicality

Before using complex algorithms, we can apply two fast screening tests:

  1. The Even Sum Rule (Handshake Lemma):
    • The sum of degrees must be even: \(\sum k_i = 2m\).
    • If the sum of a sequence is odd, it is automatically non-graphical!
  2. The Maximum Degree Bound:
    • In a simple graph of order \(n\), no node can have a degree larger than \(n-1\).
    • If any degree in the sequence exceeds \(n-1\), it is automatically non-graphical!

Graphicality: Concrete Examples

  • Graphical Sequence: \(\mathbf{d} = (4, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 1)\) with \(n = 7\)
    • Sum is \(16\) (even). Max degree is \(4 \le 6\).
    • A real simple graph can easily be constructed with these degrees.
  • Non-Graphical Sequence (Odd Sum Violation): \(\mathbf{d} = (4, 3, 3, 3, 2, 1, 1)\) with \(n = 7\)
    • Sum is \(17\) (odd). Violates Handshake Lemma.
    • No simple graph can ever have this sequence.
  • Non-Graphical Sequence (Degree Bound Violation): \(\mathbf{d} = (7, 2, 2, 1)\) with \(n = 4\)
    • Max degree is \(7\), which is greater than \(n-1 = 3\).
    • A node in a 4-person group cannot have 7 unique friends!

Average Degree

  • Average Degree (\(\bar{k}\)): The average of all node degrees in the graph.

    \[\bar{k} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} k_i = \frac{2m}{n}\]

  • It represents the expected degree of a randomly chosen node.

  • It is also directly related to density, which we will derive on the next slides!

Connecting Density & Average Degree

  • There is an intimate mathematical relationship between density (\(d\)) and average degree (\(\bar{k}\)).
  • Recall the undirected density formula: \[d = \frac{2m}{n(n-1)}\]
  • We can rewrite this by pulling out the average degree term (\(\bar{k} = \frac{2m}{n}\)): \[d = \left(\frac{2m}{n}\right) \times \frac{1}{n-1} = \bar{k} \times \frac{1}{n-1} = \frac{\bar{k}}{n-1} \qquad \iff \qquad \bar{k} = d(n-1)\]

Interpreting the Density-Degree Connection

  • Density is Average Degree Scaled by Group Size:
    • The density of an undirected graph is simply its average degree divided by the maximum possible connections of any single node (\(n-1\)).
  • For Example:
    • In a group of \(n = 10\) people, each person can have at most \(n-1 = 9\) connections.
    • If the average number of friends (\(\bar{k}\)) is \(4.5\), then the density of friendship is: \[d = \frac{4.5}{9} = 0.50 \quad (50\%)\]
  • Directed Case:
    • The exact same identity holds! Since \(d^d = \frac{m}{n(n-1)}\) and {k} is \(\frac{m}{n}\), we get: \[d^d = \frac{\bar{k}}{n-1}\]

Degree Variance

  • Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_k\)): A measure of how much the node degrees vary from the average degree.

    \[\sigma^2_k = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (k_i - \bar{k})^2\]

  • A low variance mean less inequality in connectivity (more homogeneous), while a high variance indicates more inequality (more heterogeneous).

Degree Metrics: Basic Example

  • Node Degrees: \(k_A=1, k_B=3, k_C=3, k_D=2, k_E=2, k_F=2, k_G=1\) (shown on the right)
  • Degree Set: \(\{1, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2, 1\}\) (degrees of all nodes in the graph)
  • Degree Sequence: \((3, 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1)\) (ordered from highest to lowest)
  • Degree Sum: \[\sum k_i = 1+3+3+2+2+2+1 = 14 = 2m\]

Degree Metrics: Average Degree

  • Degrees: \(k_A=1, k_B=3, k_C=3, k_D=2, k_E=2, k_F=2, k_G=1\) (shown on the right)
  • Order (\(n\)): 7 nodes
  • Average Degree (\(\bar{k}\)): \[\bar{k} = \frac{\sum k_i}{n} = \frac{14}{7} = 2.0\]

Degree Metrics: Degree Variance

  • Degrees: \(k_A=1, k_B=3, k_C=3, k_D=2, k_E=2, k_F=2, k_G=1\) (shown on the right)
  • Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_k\)): \[\sigma^2_k = \frac{\sum (k_i - \bar{k})^2}{n}\]
  • Variance Calculation: \[\sigma^2_k = \frac{3 \times (2-2)^2 + 2 \times (3-2)^2 + 2 \times (1-2)^2}{7} = \frac{4}{7} \approx 0.571\]

Regular Graphs

  • A regular graph is one where every node has the same degree \(k\) (called \(k\)-regular).
  • Since all node degrees are identical to the average, the degree variance is zero (\(\sigma^2_k = 0\)).
  • These are rare in real-world social networks but are used as baseline models.
  • Example: A 3-regular graph on 6 nodes (shown on the right).

Regular Graphs: Metrics Example

  • Node Degrees: \(k_A=3, k_B=3, k_C=3, k_D=3, k_E=3, k_F=3\) (shown on the right)
  • Order (\(n\)): 6 nodes
  • Average Degree (\(\bar{k}\)): \[\bar{k} = \frac{\sum k_i}{n} = \frac{18}{6} = 3.0\]

Regular Graphs: Variance Example

  • Average Degree (\(\bar{k}\)): 3.0 (shown on the right)
  • Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_k\)): \[\sigma^2_k = \frac{6 \times (3 - 3)^2}{6} = 0\]
  • Conclusion:
    • Since every node’s degree matches the average degree, the deviation is zero for all nodes, resulting in zero variance.

Average Nearest Neighbor Degree

  • Average Nearest Neighbor Degree (\(\bar{k}_{nn(i)}\)) measures the average degree of node \(i\)’s neighbors.
  • Formula: \[\bar{k}_{nn(i)} = \frac{1}{k_i} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} k_j\]
  • It tells us whether a node’s connections are, on average, highly connected or peripheral.

Feld’s Friendship Paradox

  • Feld’s Friendship Paradox (Scott Feld, 1991):
    • In almost all social networks, the average degree of a node’s neighbors (\(\bar{k}_{nn}\)) is greater than the average degree of the nodes in the network (\(\bar{k}\)).
  • Why does this happen?
    • Highly connected individuals (popular hubs) belong to many people’s neighborhood sets, disproportionately inflating neighbor degrees across the network.
  • Your friends, on average, have more friends than you do!

Friendship Paradox: Node Calculations (A-D)

  • For the undirected graph shown previously (average degree \(\bar{k} = 2.0\)):
    • Node A (neighbor C): \(\bar{k}_{nn(A)} = \frac{k_C}{1} = \frac{3}{1} = 3.0\)
    • Node B (neighbors C, E, F): \(\bar{k}_{nn(B)} = \frac{k_C + k_E + k_F}{3} = \frac{3 + 2 + 2}{3} \approx 2.33\)
    • Node C (neighbors A, D, B): \(\bar{k}_{nn(C)} = \frac{k_A + k_D + k_B}{3} = \frac{1 + 2 + 3}{3} = 2.0\)
    • Node D (neighbors C, G): \(\bar{k}_{nn(D)} = \frac{k_C + k_G}{2} = \frac{3 + 1}{2} = 2.0\)

Friendship Paradox: Node Calculations (E-G)

  • Continuing calculations for the remaining nodes:
    • Node E (neighbors B, F): \(\bar{k}_{nn(E)} = \frac{k_B + k_F}{2} = \frac{3 + 2}{2} = 2.5\)
    • Node F (neighbors E, B): \(\bar{k}_{nn(F)} = \frac{k_E + k_B}{2} = \frac{2 + 3}{2} = 2.5\)
    • Node G (neighbor D): \(\bar{k}_{nn(G)} = \frac{k_D}{1} = \frac{2}{1} = 2.0\)
  • Summary of average neighbor degrees:
    • \(\bar{k}_{nn} = (3.0, 2.33, 2.0, 2.0, 2.5, 2.5, 2.0)\)

Friendship Paradox: Network Average

  • From the node calculations on the previous slide, the average nearest neighbor degrees are:
    • \(\bar{k}_{nn} = (3.0, 2.33, 2.0, 2.0, 2.5, 2.5, 2.0)\)
  • Average neighbor degree for the network: \[\text{Mean}(\bar{k}_{nn}) = \frac{3.0 + 2.33 + 2.0 + 2.0 + 2.5 + 2.5 + 2.0}{7} \approx 2.33\]
  • Demonstrating the Paradox:
    • Average degree of nodes’ neighbors (\(\approx 2.33\)) is greater than the average degree of nodes (\(\bar{k} = 2.0\)).

Directed Graph Elements & Metrics

Directed Degree Sets & Sequences

  • Directed graphs have two distinct degree sets (In-Degree set and Out-Degree set) and two distinct sequences.
  • In our 6-node directed graph:
    • In-Degree Set: \(\{1, 1, 1, 3, 1, 1\}\)
    • In-Degree Sequence: \((3, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1)\)
    • Out-Degree Set: \(\{2, 3, 1, 0, 1, 1\}\)
    • Out-Degree Sequence: \((3, 2, 1, 1, 1, 0)\)

Directed Degree Range & Sum

  • Like undirected graphs, we can define the maximum, minimum, and range of degrees.
  • In directed graphs, these are defined separately for in-degree and out-degree:
    • In-Degree Range: \(k^{in}_{max} - k^{in}_{min}\)
    • Out-Degree Range: \(k^{out}_{max} - k^{out}_{min}\)
  • Degree Sum Rule:
    • The sum of in-degrees and the sum of out-degrees are both equal to the graph size \(m\) (total number of directed edges/arcs): \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} k^{in}_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} k^{out}_i = m\)

Directed Range & Sum: Example

  • For our 6-node directed graph (shown again on the right, \(m = 8\)):
    • In-Degrees: \(k^{in} = (1, 1, 1, 3, 1, 1)\)
      • \(k^{in}_{max} = 3\) (Node D), \(k^{in}_{min} = 1\)
      • Range \(k^{in}_r = 3 - 1 = 2\)
      • Sum \(\sum k^{in}_i = 1+1+1+3+1+1 = 8\)

Directed Range & Sum: Example

  • For our 6-node directed graph (shown again on the right, \(m = 8\)):
    • Out-Degrees: \(k^{out} = (2, 3, 1, 0, 1, 1)\)
      • \(k^{out}_{max} = 3\) (Node B), \(k^{out}_{min} = 0\) (Node D)
      • Range \(k^{out}_r = 3 - 0 = 3\)
      • Sum \(\sum k^{out}_i = 2+3+1+0+1+1 = 8\)

Directed Average Degree

  • Average In-Degree (\(\bar{k}^{in}\)) and Average Out-Degree (\(\bar{k}^{out}\)): \(\bar{k}^{in} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} k^{in}_i \quad \text{and} \quad \bar{k}^{out} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} k^{out}_i\)
  • The Equivalence Theorem:
    • In every directed graph, the average in-degree is exactly equal to the average out-degree: \(\bar{k}^{in} = \bar{k}^{out} = \frac{m}{n}\)
    • Why? Each directed edge \(u \rightarrow v\) adds exactly 1 to the out-degree of \(u\) and exactly 1 to the in-degree of \(v\).

Equivalence of Means: Example

  • In our 6-node directed graph:
    • Order (\(n\)): 6 nodes
    • Size (\(m\)): 8 directed edges
  • Mean In-Degree: \(\bar{k}^{in} = \frac{1+1+1+3+1+1}{6} = \frac{8}{6} \approx 1.33\)
  • Mean Out-Degree: \(\bar{k}^{out} = \frac{2+3+1+0+1+1}{6} = \frac{8}{6} \approx 1.33\)
  • They are equal! (\(\bar{k}^{in} = \bar{k}^{out} = 1.33 = m/n\))

Directed Degree Variance

  • While the means are always equal, the variances of in-degree and out-degree can be completely different!
  • In-Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_{in}\)): \(\sigma^2_{in} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (k^{in}_i - \bar{k}^{in})^2\)
    • Sociological meaning: Measures inequality in prestige/popularity (do a few nodes receive all ties?).
  • Out-Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_{out}\)): \(\sigma^2_{out} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (k^{out}_i - \bar{k}^{out})^2\)
    • Sociological meaning: Measures inequality in sociability/activity (do some nodes send way more ties than others?).

Directed Degree Variance: Example

  • In our 6-node directed graph:
    • Mean degree (\(\bar{k}\)) is \(\approx 1.33\)
  • In-Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_{in}\)):
    • Values:
      • \(k^{in} = (1, 1, 1, 3, 1, 1)\)
    • Calculation:
      • \(\frac{5 \times (1-1.33)^2 + 1 \times (3-1.33)^2}{6} =\)
      • \(\frac{5 \times 0.11 + 2.78}{6} \approx 0.556\)

Directed Degree Variance: Example

  • In our 6-node directed graph:
    • Mean degree (\(\bar{k}\)) is \(\approx 1.33\)
  • Out-Degree Variance (\(\sigma^2_{out}\)):
    • Values:
      • \(k^{out} = (2, 3, 1, 0, 1, 1)\)
    • Calculation:
      • \(\frac{(2-1.33)^2 + (3-1.33)^2 + 3(1-1.33)^2 + (0-1.33)^2}{6} \approx 0.889\)
  • Out-degree is more heterogeneous than in-degree in this network!

Comparing Density: Undirected vs. Directed

  • Maximum Possible Edges:
    • Undirected: \(Max(E)_{undir} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}\)
    • Directed: \(Max(E)_{dir} = n(n - 1)\)
  • Density Scaling:
    • Because a directed graph can hold exactly twice as many edges as an undirected graph of the same order: \(d_{dir} = \frac{1}{2} d_{undir} \quad \text{for the same } m \text{ and } n\)
    • Directed density is always more “conservative”—it requires twice as many edges as an undirected graph to reach the same level of density!

Reciprocity in Graphs

  • In an undirected graph, reciprocity is trivial (\(100\%\)):
    • Every connection is mutual by definition (\(u - v \implies v - u\)).
  • In a directed graph, reciprocity is a key structural metric:
    • It measures the extent to which directed relations are sent back and forth (\(u \rightarrow v \implies v \rightarrow u\)).
    • High reciprocity is a signature of trust, mutual support, and stable social structure.
  • We measure reciprocity using two standard metrics: Arc-based and Dyad-based reciprocity.

Reciprocity Metrics: Formulas

  • Arc-based Reciprocity (\(r_{arc}\)):
    • The proportion of all directed edges (arcs) that are reciprocal: \(r_{arc} = \frac{2 \times D_{rec}}{m}\)
  • Dyad-based Reciprocity (\(r_{dyad}\)):
    • The proportion of connected vertex pairs (dyads) that are reciprocal: \(r_{dyad} = \frac{D_{rec}}{D_{rec} + D_{asym}}\)

Reciprocity Calculation: Example

  • Let’s calculate reciprocity for our 6-node digraph (\(m = 8\)):
    • Reciprocal (Mutual) Dyad: \(\{A, B\}\) (1 dyad, \(2\) arcs)
    • Non-Reciprocal (Asymmetric) Dyads: \(\{B, C\}, \{B, D\}, \{E, D\}, \{G, D\}, \{A, E\}, \{C, G\}\) (6 dyads, \(6\) arcs)
    • Total connected dyads: \(1 + 6 = 7\)
  • Arc-based reciprocity (igraph "default"): \(r_{arc} = \frac{2 \times 1}{8} = 0.250 \quad (25.0\%)\)
  • Dyad-based reciprocity (igraph "ratio"): \(r_{dyad} = \frac{1}{1 + 6} = \frac{1}{7} \approx 0.143 \quad (14.3\%)\)
