Theories of Valenced Interactions

Karma, Status, Solidarity, and Dominance in Sentiment Networks

Part 1: Valenced Interactions & Karma Theory

Introduction to Valenced Interactions

Beyond Simple Co-membership

  • In social network analysis, we move beyond simple presence or absence of links to examine sentiment networks.
  • Relationships have an emotional and evaluative character, labeled as valence:
    • Positive valence (e.g., liking, helping, admiring).
    • Negative valence (e.g., disliking, bullying, fighting).
  • Valenced Interactions are governed by deep-seated psychological micro-motives.
  • These principles drive how networks grow, change, and cohere into stable macrostructures.

Karma Theory: Core Concept

Direct Reciprocity and Sentiment Feedback

  • Core Principle: “You get what you spread out!”
  • Proposes that individuals tend to reciprocate the sentiments that others broadcast to the network.
  • Predicts received valenced indegree (what you receive) based on expressed valenced outdegree (what you send out).
  • Illustrates common expressions:
    • “Likers get liked” (positive reciprocity).
    • “Bullies get bullied” (negative reciprocity).

Karma Theory: Network-Wide Flow

  • Karma Flows can be generalized across broader spheres in the network.
  • Positive Sphere: Node A (positive spreader) actively initiates friendly ties and receives deference/support in return from the network.
  • Negative Sphere: Node B (negative spreader) actively initiates hostile ties and attracts reciprocal conflict/bullying from multiple alters.
  • Establishes separate, cohesive emotional regions.

Karma Dynamics: Virtuous vs. Vicious Cycles

  • Karma principles drive valenced networks to grow and evolve via feedback loops:
  • Virtuous Cycles: An initial positive act (\(+\)) breeds positive reciprocity (\(+\)). These positive feedback loops lock actors into mutually reinforcing, cooperative cliques.
  • Vicious Cycles: A single unprovoked negative act (\(-\)) breeds hostile counter-attacks (\(-\)). This triggers cascading negative spirals, spreading localized conflict throughout the network.
  • Over time, outdegree and received indegree valences converge on a node-by-node basis.

Part 2: Status Theory

Status Theory: Introduction

  • Status Theory proposes that sentiments towards an alter are heavily driven by how others feel about them.
  • This represents a social prestige alignment mechanism:
    • If someone is liked by many, you align your sentiment and like them too.
    • If someone is disliked by many, you align your sentiment and dislike them.
  • A node’s status is determined by the valence signs of incoming ties:
    • High status = receiving many positive links.
    • Low status = receiving many negative links.

Prestige and Deference Alignment

  • Inside status-seeking systems, tie directions carry implicit status expectations:
    • Directed positive tie from Ego to Alter \(\implies\) Ego considers Alter to have higher status (Deference).
    • Directed negative tie from Ego to Alter \(\implies\) Ego considers Alter to have lower status (Dominance).
  • Status-seeking actors strive to align their sentiments with prestige to avoid losing their own social standing.
  • Node labels are shortened (Ego, High, Low) to fit node markers perfectly.

Status and Outgoing Sentiments

  • Your outgoing ties affect your own social position:
    • Status Gain:
      • Sending positive ties to high-status actors (basking in reflected glory).
      • Sending negative ties to low-status actors (distancing from pariahs).
    • Status Loss:
      • Sending positive ties to low-status actors (guilt by association).
      • Sending negative ties to high-status actors (hostility to elites backfires).
  • Actors actively prune or withdraw ties that cause status loss.

Status Dynamics: Centralization and Hierarchy Growth

  • How status networks grow and change over time:
    • Preferential Deference: High-status nodes attract a disproportionate share of incoming positive ties over time (a “rich-get-richer” effect).
    • Dynamic Pruning: High-status nodes lose their negative ties, while low-status nodes lose their positive ties.
    • “Gang-up” Effect: Subordinates consolidate negative ties onto low-status pariahs to boost their own standings.
    • Hierarchy Emergence: Sentiments organize into highly centralized, strictly ordered prestige rankings.

Part 3: Solidarity Theory

Solidarity Theory: Core Concept

  • Central idea: “Those in similar positions stick together!”
  • We feel positively towards others who are similarly situated within the sentiment network, and negatively towards those who are differently situated.
  • Shared position is measured based on the status of incoming ties (both popular or both unpopular).

Measuring Shared Position

  • Structural similarity acts as a strong motivator for positive bonding:
    • Status Solidarity: Elite, popular actors share a similar high status, motivating them to like and support one another (“popular kids stick together”).
    • Unpopularity Solidarity: Disliked, downtrodden actors share a similar lower-tier position, motivating them to bond (“downtrodden stick together”).
    • Vertical Cleavages: Since different positions motivate negative sentiments, positive ties spanning across tiers are actively pruned.

Solidarity Dynamics: Stratification and Clique Growth

  • How Solidarity networks grow and stratify over time:
    • Horizontal Integration: Structural equivalence drives the creation of reciprocal positive ties within the same tier (clique formation).
    • Class Cohesion: Solidarity processes stabilize clusters into horizontal social classes (e.g., unions, associations, exclusive clubs).
    • Vertical Hostility: Cross-tier positive ties are severed, and negative ties (deference/dominance) solidify vertical boundaries.
    • Clique Stratification: The network evolves into a horizontally integrated, vertically polarized system.

Part 4: Dominance Hierarchies

Dominance Orders in the Animal Kingdom

  • Dominance hierarchies are observed across many species in the animal kingdom — most notably among chickens. You do not need a big brain to create a complex social network!
  • A “pure” dominance order has three algebraic properties:
    • Directed: a tie means “i dominates j.”
    • Antisymmetric: if A dominates B, B cannot dominate A.
    • Complete: for every pair, someone dominates — either A dominates B or B dominates A.

Transitivity in Dominance Orders

  • Transitivity: dominance passes through nodes.
  • If A dominates B, and B dominates C, then A also dominates C — just like \(A > B\) and \(B > C\) imply \(A > C\).
  • In a directed network: if A is above B, A is above everyone B is above.
  • A linear dominance order must be transitive — the A → C arc has to exist.

Vacuous Transitivity

  • What if A → B, but B does not direct a tie to C?
  • The precondition for transitivity — A → B and B → C — is not met.
  • The triad cannot violate transitivity, because the necessary chain does not exist.
  • This configuration is vacuously transitive:
    • It does not actively demonstrate transitivity…
    • …but it does not violate it either.
  • Only triads with a full chain A → B → C can be transitive or intransitive.

Perfectly Linear Hierarchies: The Pecking Order

  • Chickens placed in a new environment scuffle until a clear pecking order emerges.
  • The winner asserts dominance; this continues until every chicken knows its place.
  • Alpha dominates Beta and everyone below; Beta dominates Gamma and everyone below, and so on.
  • The hierarchy is ritually reinforced: the higher-status chicken pecks, the lower-status one assents.
  • A classic perfectly transitive dominance order.

Human Hierarchies: Deviations from Perfect Transitivity

  • Unlike chickens, human hierarchies are typically not perfectly transitive — and they vary by context.
  • A teacher has final authority in the classroom, but those relations may not apply outside it.
  • Deference to a President can invert during an election — the position depends on the voters’ will.
  • Two people can each lead in different clubs; their relative standing shifts with the decision at hand.
  • Humans tacitly recognize orderings, but disagreements over what determines status arise, and hierarchies are context-dependent.
  • Still, human networks can be analyzed by comparing them to these idealized structures.
  • Human hierarchies still tend toward the linear ideal:
    • Deviations are often temporary or local.
    • Ambiguity about the order itself breeds conflict and competition.

Seven Triad Types in Dominance Networks

  • A dominance network can contain seven possible triadic configurations built entirely of asymmetric or null ties.
  • For a linear order to exist, it must be transitive and contain no cyclical triads.

Models of Hierarchy Formation: The Attribute-Based Model

  • Attribute-based model: position in the hierarchy reflects inherent qualities and abilities — talent, athletic ability, humor, being a good friend all count as “quality” — with the “biggest, fastest, strongest” rising to the top.
  • Deference does the compensating (Gould 2002):
    • A less talented person “exchanges” deference for association with a more talented one.
    • Yielding signals the recipient’s quality and status, increasing their influence.
  • If required deference exceeds the acceptable limits of reciprocity, the tie may not form at all.
  • Implication: a relatively stable hierarchy mirroring underlying capabilities.
  • But is rank really just a readout of attributes?
  • Chicken hierarchies break down at about \(N = 10\) — perfect linearity is too good to be a simple attribute ranking.
  • Enter the interaction-based model…

The Interaction-Based Model: Agonistic Competition

  • Interaction-based model (Chase 1982): hierarchies emerge from the sequence and outcomes of past agonistic encounters — not only from attributes.
  • Winner effects: winning brings confidence, experience, testosterone — winners dominate again (double-dominant triads).
  • Loser effects: losing brings lowered confidence and injury — losers lose again (double-loser triads).
  • Bystander effects: observers update from watching:
    • A bystander defers to the winner and rarely dominates them.
    • But a bystander becomes stronger relative to the loser, and rarely gets dominated by them.

Why Iterative Competition Guarantees Linearity

  • Combine the three effects over repeated contests:
    • Winners keep winning; losers keep losing (double-dominant and double-loser triads).
    • Bystanders almost never dominate the initial dominant — and almost never get dominated by the initial loser.
  • Bystander updating therefore locks in the outcome of every observed fight rather than reversing it.
  • Step by step, every triad that could be cyclical or intransitive gets resolved toward transitivity.
  • The resulting hierarchy is not a pure readout of attributes: it is shaped by the history and sequence of interactions.
  • Iterative, social competition guarantees transitivity and linearity in the dominance order over time.

Part 5: Comparative Summary & Synthesis

Comparison of Karma, Status, and Solidarity

  • Compare how distinct social-psychological principles drive micro-motives.
  • Each theory assumes different levels of agency and structural awareness:
    • Karma: Focuses strictly on dyadic reciprocity (actions sent vs. received).
    • Status: Aligns sentiments with prestige hierarchies (prestige seeking).
    • Solidarity: Aligns sentiments with structural equivalence (horizontal classes).

Co-Evolution of Valenced Networks: A Combined Model

  • In real-world sentiment networks, these three principles do not act in isolation; they interact and co-evolve:
    1. Phase 1: Raw Interaction: Initial encounters are governed by Karma (direct reciprocity loops).
    2. Phase 2: Hierarchy Emergence: As actors seek status and prestige, Status seeking creates centralized deference paths.
    3. Phase 3: Factional Stratification: Structural equivalence triggers Solidarity, freezing actors into cohesive horizontal classes.
  • Explains how complex social stratifications emerge from simple micro-motives.
