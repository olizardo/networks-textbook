Graph Theory: Paths, Walks & Directed Connections

The Basics of Social Network Analysis

Part 1: Indirect Connections & Paths

Adjacency vs. Indirect Connections

  • Adjacency captures direct connections between nodes:
    • If \(A\) is adjacent to \(B\), they share an edge.
  • But many network processes operate indirectly:
    • We must look beyond immediate contacts to the “friends of our friends.”
    • Diffusion Channels: Indirect connections act as channels for gossip, information transmission, disease contagion, or social influence.
  • In graph theory, indirect connections are formalized using three core concepts:
    • Path, Connectivity, and Reachability.

What is a Path?

  • A path between \(u\) and \(v\) is an alternating sequence of nodes and edges that:
    • Begins with an origin node (\(u\)).
    • Ends with a destination node (\(v\)).
    • Does not repeat any nodes (which implies edges are not repeated either!).
  • Anatomy of a Path (e.g. Red path from \(B\) to \(D\) on the right):
    • Written as: \(B-D = \{BE, ED\}\) (length \(l = 2\)).
    • Origin: \(B\), Destination: \(D\) (together called the end nodes).
    • Inner Nodes: \(E\) (the nodes in between).

Multiple Paths & Geodesics

  • Multiple distinct paths can connect the same pair of nodes.
  • Let’s trace paths from \(B\) to \(A\) (the graph cycles through each as it appears):
  • \(P_1 = \{BE, ED, DA\} \quad (l = 3)\)
  • \(P_2 = \{BE, EC, CA\} \quad (l = 3)\)
  • \(P_3 = \{BE, ED, DC, CA\} \quad (l = 4)\)
  • \(P_4 = \{BE, EC, CD, DA\} \quad (l = 4)\)
  • Shortest Path (Geodesic):
    • The path of minimum length between two nodes.
    • Geodesic Distance: The length of the geodesic path (here, geodesic distance \(d(B, A) = 3\), with tied geodesics \(P_1\) and \(P_2\)).

Multiple Shortest Paths (Geodesic Ties)

  • In real networks, geodesic distances are often tied:
    • There can be multiple distinct shortest paths of equal minimum length connecting the exact same pair of nodes.
  • Example: Shortest paths from \(B\) to \(A\) in our undirected graph:
    • Both paths have a geodesic distance of \(d(B, A) = 3\).
    • Geodesic Path 1: \(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A\) (Bottom)
    • Let’s visualize this path highlighted in red on the right.

Geodesic Ties: Redundancy & Reliability

  • Let’s look at the second geodesic path from \(B\) to \(A\):
    • Geodesic Path 2: \(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow C \rightarrow A\) (Top)
    • Let’s visualize this path highlighted in red on the right.
  • Redundancy and Reliability:
    • Having multiple geodesics of equal minimum length provides alternative routes of maximum efficiency.
    • Structural Robustness: If a node or edge on one path fails (e.g. node \(D\)), communication is completely unaffected because Path 2 remains open!

Sociological Significance of Geodesics

  • Why do social scientists care so much about shortest paths (geodesics) in real networks?
  • 1. Speed and Bandwidth of Transmission:
    • Information, trust, and influence decay as they travel along long paths.
    • Geodesic paths represent the channels through which diffusion (of gossip, innovation, or virus) happens fastest and with least loss.
  • 2. Power, Brokerage, and Betweenness:
    • Actors who lie on the geodesics of many other pairs of nodes in the network play a major role as information brokers or gatekeepers.
    • They control information flow, filter messages, and can extract rents or gain social influence (this is the basis of Betweenness Centrality).
  • 3. Search and the “Small World” Phenomenon:
    • Stanley Milgram’s classic “six degrees of separation” experiment showed that human social networks have surprisingly short geodesic distances.
    • Even in massive networks of millions of people, any two individuals are connected by incredibly short paths of friendship!

Node Eccentricity

  • Definition: The eccentricity of a node is the maximum geodesic distance from that node to any other node in the network.
    • It measures the “worst-case” distance to reach any other actor.
  • Example 1: Node E (Central Hub):
    • Distances from \(E\): \(d(E, B) = 1\), \(d(E, C) = 1\), \(d(E, D) = 1\), \(d(E, A) = 2\).
    • The maximum distance is \(2\) (to node \(A\)).
    • Eccentricity of \(E\) = 2.
  • Example 2: Node B (Peripheral Leaf):
    • Distances from \(B\): \(d(B, E) = 1\), \(d(B, C) = 2\), \(d(B, D) = 2\), \(d(B, A) = 3\).
    • The maximum distance is \(3\) (to node \(A\)).
    • Eccentricity of \(B\) = 3.

Radius and Diameter

  • Eccentricity describes a single node; two measures summarize the eccentricities of the whole graph:
  • Radius \(\mathrm{rad}(G)\): The minimum eccentricity across all nodes.
    • Here, \(\mathrm{rad}(G) = 2\), attained by \(C\), \(D\), and \(E\) — the center of the network.
  • Diameter \(\mathrm{diam}(G)\): The maximum eccentricity across all nodes.
    • Here, \(\mathrm{diam}(G) = 3\), attained by \(A\) and \(B\) — the periphery of the network.
  • The red path on the right is a diametral path: a geodesic whose length equals the diameter (\(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A\), \(l = 3\)).

What is a Cycle?

  • A cycle is a path of length 3 or larger that starts and ends on the same node.
  • Key Concepts:
    • Origin and destination are identical (the same vertex).
    • All inner nodes and all edges must be completely unique.
    • Cycles are the fundamental building blocks of network redundancy and feedback loops.

Visualizing a Simple Cycle

  • Let’s look at a simple cycle highlighted in our graph:
    • Cycle: \(C \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow C\).
    • It has a length of 3 (consisting of 3 edges).
  • Verification of Properties:
    • Origin and destination are identical (both are node \(C\)).
    • All intermediate nodes (\(D\), \(A\)) are unique.
    • All traversed edges are unique.
    • This closed loop creates a fundamental feedback pathway.

Graph Girth

  • Definition: The girth of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle.
  • Properties:
    • If a graph contains no cycles at all (like a tree graph), its girth is defined as infinity (\(\infty\)).
    • For our 5-node undirected graph on the right, the shortest cycle is the triangle: \[E \rightarrow D \rightarrow C \rightarrow E\]
    • Thus, the girth of this graph is 3.
  • Sociological Note: The girth indexes how tight the smallest closed circle of actors is — a girth of 3 means friends-of-friends are directly tied, the signature of strong local closure (Simmelian ties).

Graph Circumference

  • Definition: The circumference of a graph is the length of its longest cycle.
  • Properties:
    • If a graph is acyclic, its circumference is defined as zero or undefined.
    • For our 5-node undirected graph on the right, the longest cycle is the four-loop: \[E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow C \rightarrow E\]
    • Thus, the circumference of this graph is 4.
  • Sociological Note: The circumference captures the longest chain of distinct actors that closes a feedback loop — a gauge of how far redundancy can stretch across a network.

Special Paths: Hamiltonian Paths

  • Hamiltonian Path: A path that visits every single node in the graph exactly once.
  • Our Example Graph: Shown on the right, it has 5 nodes \(\{A, B, C, D, E\}\).
  • A Hamiltonian Path: Let’s trace one in red: \[A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E\]
    • Every node is visited exactly once.
    • The diagonal edge \(B-E\) remains untraversed.
  • Sociological Note: Hamiltonian structure matters whenever a chain of introductions must reach every actor exactly once — the logic behind round-robin designs and snowball-sampling routes that avoid revisits.

Special Paths: Hamiltonian Cycles

  • Hamiltonian Cycle: A cycle that visits every single node in the graph exactly once and returns to the origin.
  • Our Hamiltonian Cycle: Let’s trace it in red on the right: \[A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E \rightarrow A\]
    • This cycle forms the complete outer pentagonal ring.
    • Visits all 5 nodes exactly once and closes the loop!
    • Degree Condition: Every node has degree \(\ge 2\) — a necessary condition for a Hamiltonian cycle (though not, by itself, sufficient).

Part 2: Walks, Trails, and Directed Connections

Walks, Trails, and Paths

  • Walk: Any sequence of alternating nodes and edges connecting \(u\) to \(v\).
    • No restrictions: Nodes and edges can be repeated as much as you want!
    • e.g., \(\{B \rightarrow E \rightarrow C \rightarrow E \rightarrow D\}\) (traverses \(E\) and edge \(E-C\) twice).
  • Trail: A walk that does not repeat any edges.
    • Repeated nodes are allowed, but every edge in the sequence must be unique.
    • e.g., \(\{B \rightarrow E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow C \rightarrow D\}\) (repeats node \(D\) but all edges are unique).
  • Path: A walk that does not repeat any nodes (which means edges cannot be repeated either).
  • The Nested Hierarchy: All paths are trails, and all trails are walks, but the reverse is not true: \[Paths \subset Trails \subset Walks\]

Walks: A Visual Example

  • Walk (B-E-C-E-D):
    • We start at node \(B\), walk to \(E\), then \(C\), then back to \(E\), and finally to \(D\).
    • Repeated Node: Node \(E\) is visited twice in this sequence.
    • Repeated Edge: Edge \(E-C\) (and \(C-E\)) is traversed twice.
    • Highlighted in red on the right.

Trails: A Visual Example

  • Trail (B-E-D-A-C-D):
    • We start at \(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow C \rightarrow D\).
    • Repeated Node: Node \(D\) is visited twice.
    • Repeated Edges: None! Every edge in the sequence is completely unique.
    • Highlighted in red on the right.

Part 3: Directed Paths, Reachability & Cycles

Paths in Directed Graphs (Digraphs)

  • In directed graphs, paths must strictly respect the direction of the arrows!
  • Let’s trace directed paths from \(A\) to \(B\) in our digraph (the graph cycles through each as it appears):
  • \(P_1 = \{AC, CE, EB\} \quad (l = 3)\)
  • \(P_2 = \{AD, DE, EB\} \quad (l = 3)\)
  • \(P_3 = \{AC, CD, DE, EB\} \quad (l = 4)\)
  • But can we find any directed path from \(B\) to \(A\)?
    • No! All arrows lead away from \(A\) and toward \(B\).

Directed Reachability

  • Asymmetric Reachability:
    • Node \(A\) can reach \(B\) via directed path \(\{AD, DE, EB\}\) (\(l_{AB} = 3\)).
    • But node \(B\) cannot reach \(A\) because we cannot travel against the arrows (\(l_{BA} = \infty\)).
  • Mutual Reachability:
    • Two nodes \(u\) and \(v\) are mutually reachable if there exists a directed path from \(u \rightarrow v\) AND from \(v \rightarrow u\).
    • These two paths do not have to be the same length!

Mutual Reachability: Example

  • Example: Nodes \(B\) and \(C\) are mutually reachable (shown on the right):
    • \(B\) reaches \(C\) via \(\{BE, ED, DC\} \quad (l = 3)\)
    • \(C\) reaches \(B\) via shortest path \(\{CE, EB\} \quad (l = 2)\)
  • Note:
    • The two paths have different lengths (\(3\) vs. \(2\)) — mutual reachability does not require equal travel times in both directions!

Directed Cycles & DAGs

  • A directed cycle is a cycle where all arrows flow in a single, continuous direction.
    • Example: Cycle starting and ending at \(C\):
      • \(\{CE, ED, DC\} \quad (l = 3)\)
  • Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs):
    • Directed graphs that contain no directed cycles.
    • e.g. strict organizational command structures, directed trees.
    • Anti-symmetric relations (like “is boss of”) structurally forbid cycles to maintain consistent hierarchy.

Ignoring the Arrows: Semipaths

  • What if we want to trace connections in a directed graph but ignore the arrows?
  • This is called a semipath (or semicycle if it is a closed loop).
  • A semipath treats directed arcs as undirected edges.
  • Example: Semipath connecting \(B\) and \(A\):
    • \(\{BE, EC, CA\}\) (highlighted in red on the right).
