Defining Social Networks

  • Networks are everywhere, but only a specific subset of them is actually a social network.
  • A network is “social” if it involves people (or groups) and their thoughts, interactions, and behaviors.
  • Even when we connect through technology (like texting or flying), it is the human interaction that makes it a social network.
  • In this class, we focus on the “purely social” elements: relationships, feelings, and how people exchange things.

The Roots of Social Network Analysis (SNA)

  • Social Network Analysis (SNA) is the main way we study these connections.
  • It grew primarily in the field of sociology, starting in the 1970s.
  • Today it is a team effort across different sciences, drawing on insights from multiple disciplines:
    • Sociology
    • Psychology
    • Anthropology
    • Communication

What is a social network analysis?

  • SNA is an interdisciplinary field in social science in charge of studying social networks.

The Big Picture: Network Science

  • SNA is just one branch of a much larger tree called Network Science.
  • Network Science studies all kinds of networks in the world.
  • This broader field includes experts from physics, computer science, biology, data science, and math.

The Big Picture: Network Science

Summary

  • Networks: Systems of entities linked by relations
    • Different kinds: Social, biological, technological, etc.
  • Social Networks: Systems of interactive people (or groups of people) linked by social relations
  • Network Science: Interdisciplinary field in charge of studying all types of networks
    • Biology, mathematics, computer science, physics, etc.
  • Social Network Analysis: Interdisciplinary field in charge of studying social networks
    • Sociology, psychology, anthropology, communication

The two faces of Social Network Analysis

  • SNA has two main faces; the first is Network Theory.
  • Network Theory explains how networks work and what they do to the people inside them.
  • It tries to answer big questions: Why do people connect? How do they behave? What are the consequences?
  • A great example is “social capital”—the idea that who you know brings you real-world benefits.

The two faces of Social Network Analysis

  • The second side of SNA is Network Measurement.
  • This is where we use math and numbers to measure network properties.
  • Math helps us take “fuzzy” social concepts—like “popularity” or “social position”—and turn them into precise, measurable quantities.
  • These numbers allow us to predict why some people, companies, or countries succeed while others do not.

The two faces of social network analysis

  • Theory: General statements about how people behave in networks.
  • Measurement: Helps make natural language concepts more precise.

Levels of Analysis in social networks

We will be working through all these levels in the course! (But not in this order)

Zooming In: Micro Levels of Analysis

  • We can study networks by zooming in very closely.
  • The Individual (Node) Level: We examine a single person and their personal connections, collectively known as their “ego network”.
  • Small Configurations (Dyad/Triad Level): We look at relationships between two or three people.
    • At this level, we study things like how strong a tie is, or how small groups balance their friendships.

Zooming Out: Macro Levels of Analysis

  • Communities (Subgroups): We look at larger clusters of people to see how close-knit they are.
  • The Whole Network: We look at the entire system to find large-scale trends, like whether the network is a “Small World”.

Three ways of talking about “social networks”

  • Social network theory is a branch of social science that uses natural language and intuition to describe real-world human interactions.
    • Its concepts can be vague.
  • Graph theory is a branch of pure mathematics that uses less ambiguous terms to define “analogues” of these fuzzy social network concepts.
  • Matrix algebra is a branch of applied mathematics that links graph theory concepts to allow for calculations and the development of metrics for social network ideas (like “centrality”).

The “Three-Step Shuffle”

  • A major goal of learning SNA is mastering three different ways to view networks:
    1. Seeing networks as real-world human interactions.
    2. Seeing networks as mathematical graphs.
    3. Seeing networks as quantitative data tables (matrices).
  • Transitioning smoothly between these three views is the key to understanding how networks work and change.
