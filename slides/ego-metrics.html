Measuring Ego Networks

Ego Network Metrics: Size, Effective Size, Composition, Homophily, Diversity, and Clustering

Ego Networks: The Basic Ingredients

  • Ego: the central node whose network we are describing.
  • Alters: the nodes surrounding ego.
  • Ego-alter ties are obligatory: every alter is connected to ego.
  • Alter-alter ties are optional: alters may or may not know one another.

Schematic example; not data from a particular study.

The Two Subgraphs of an Ego Network

  • The star subgraph contains all the ego-to-alter ties (\(E_{ea}\)).
  • The alter-alter subgraph is the local network among the people who are connected to ego.
  • Alter set: \(V = \{a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h\}\)
  • Alter-alter ties: \(E = \{ab, ah, ag, bh, bg, de, dg, ef, fg, gh\}\)

Ego Network Data: What You Need

There are four pieces of information we need to compute the basic ego-network properties:

  1. The number of ego-alter ties — the same as the number of alters!
  1. The number of alter-alter ties.
  1. Sociodemographic characteristics of ego: age, gender, racial identity, class, education.
  1. Sociodemographic characteristics of alters: the same attributes, for each alter.

Effective Size: The Idea

  • Still tells us whether your ego network is large or small.
  • But discounts alters who are connected to one another as redundant.
  • Given by ego-network size minus the average degree of the alter-alter subgraph:

\[ES = S - \frac{2m}{n}\]

\[ES = 8 - \frac{28}{8} = 8 - 3.5 = 4.5\]

\[ES = 8 - \frac{14}{8} = 8 - 1.75 = 6.25\]

Composition

  • Tells us whether your ego network is dominated by one category of alters.
  • Given by the proportion of alters who belong to a category: kin, friends, neighbors, co-workers; gender, race, education.

\[Prop(Kin) = \frac{\text{number of kin alters}}{S}\]

  • Here: \(Prop(Kin) = 5/8 = 0.63\) — a kin-heavy ego network.

Composition: Another Example

  • Composition can be computed for any category the alters fall into.

  • The same network by gender identity:

    • \(Prop(Men) = 5/8 = 0.63\)
    • \(Prop(Women) = 2/8 = 0.25\)
    • \(Prop(Nonbinary) = 1/8 \approx 0.13\)
  • Same take: the network is predominantly male.

Homophily: The E-I Index

  • Tells us whether the people in your ego network are the same or different from you on some sociodemographic characteristic: age, race, gender, education.
  • Example: ego identifies as Black. Alters shaded the same color as ego share ego’s identity (“internal”); all others are “external.”
  • \(E\) = number of “External” alters (different from you); \(I\) = number of “Internal” alters (same as you).

\[EI = \frac{E - I}{E + I}\]

Homophily: Worked Example and Interpretation

  • Here: \(E = 6\), \(I = 2\) (alters b and f are Black, like ego).

\[EI = \frac{6 - 2}{6 + 2} = \frac{4}{8} = +0.5\]

  • Negative EI: preference for “internal” ties. An EI of \(-1.0\) is maximum homophily.
  • Positive EI: preference for “external” ties. An EI of \(+1.0\) is maximum heterophily.
  • EI = 0: no preference — equal numbers of internal and external ties. Here, ego’s ties lean external.

Diversity: Blau’s Index

  • Tells us whether you tend to connect with a wide range of others on some sociodemographic characteristic.
  • Given by Blau’s index of heterogeneity (\(H\)):

\[H = 1 - \sum_{k} p_k^2\]

  • Steps:

    1. Compute the proportion \(p_k\) of alters in each group \(k\).
    2. Square each proportion and sum them.
    3. Subtract from 1.0.

Diversity: Worked Example

  • Eight alters: 3 White, 2 Black, 2 Asian, 1 Hispanic.

\[H = 1 - \left(\tfrac{3}{8}^2 + \tfrac{2}{8}^2 + \tfrac{1}{8}^2 + \tfrac{2}{8}^2\right)\]

\[H = 1 - 0.28 = 0.72\]

  • Interpretation:

    • A completely homogeneous ego network has \(H = 0\) — no diversity among alters.
    • The more diverse the ego network, the closer \(H\) is to 1.0.
    • \(H\) is at a maximum (\(H_{max} = 1 - 1/k\)) when alters are spread evenly across \(k\) groups. With \(k = 4\): \(H_{max} = 0.75\), so \(H/H_{max} = 0.96\).

Clustering

  • Tells us the extent to which your alters are interconnected among themselves (know one another) versus strangers to one another.

  • Given by the clustering coefficient (\(CC_i\)). You need:

    • The number of alter-alter ties (\(m\))
    • The number of alters (\(n\))
  • Don’t care about the ego-alter links!

\[CC_i = \frac{2m}{n(n-1)}\]

Clustering: A Clustered Example

  • \(n = 8\) alters; \(m = 7\) alter-alter ties.

\[CC_i = \frac{2 \times 7}{8 \times 7} = \frac{14}{56} = 0.25\]

  • The ties are concentrated: two tight triangles of mutual friends, joined by a single bridge.
  • The clustering coefficient is the density of the network formed among your alters — the probability that two randomly chosen people you know also know one another.

Clustering: A Less Clustered Example

  • Same number of alters (\(n = 8\)), but only \(m = 4\) alter-alter ties.

\[CC_i = \frac{2 \times 4}{8 \times 7} = \frac{8}{56} = 0.14\]

  • The ties are scattered: four disjoint pairs, no triangles at all.
  • Same-size ego networks can differ sharply in how concentrated their alter-alter ties are — and the clustering coefficient picks this up.

Clustering: The Extremes

CC = 0.0 (\(m = 0\)): none of your friends know one another.

CC = 1.0 (\(m = 28\)): every one of your friends knows one another.

Summary: Measuring Ego Networks

Measure Question
Size How many alters are in the network?
Effective size How large is it net of redundant alters?
Composition Is it dominated by one category of alters?
Homophily (E-I) How similar are alters to ego?
Diversity (H) How varied are the attributes of the alters?
Clustering (CC) To what extent do alters know one another?
  • Choosing the right metric depends entirely on your research question.
  • Homogeneity and diversity are distinct: an ego network can be high on one and low on the other, depending on how the groups are defined.
  • Ego clustering measures network density, showing whether an ego is embedded in a single tight-knit circle or bridges separate, unconstrained social worlds.
