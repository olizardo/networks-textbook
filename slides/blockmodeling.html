Graph Theory: Blockmodeling

Role Structure and Block Relations

Part 1: The Correlation Distance & CONCOR

The Correlation Distance (\(d^{corr}_{k,l}\))

  • Beyond Euclidean distance, we can measure structural similarity using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (\(r_{kl}\) or \(d^{corr}_{kl}\)) between node vectors.
  • Compares row (or column) vectors and returns a value between \(-1.0\) and \(+1.0\):
    • \(+1.0\): Nodes are perfectly structurally equivalent.
    • Positive (\(> 0.0\)): Nodes are structurally similar.
    • Negative (\(< 0.0\)): Nodes are structurally dissimilar.
    • \(-1.0\): Nodes are completely different, pointing to completely disjoint sets of neighbors.
  • The formula is:
    • \[d^{corr}_{k, l} = \frac{\sum_j (a_{(k)j} - \overline{a}_{(k)j}) \times (a_{(l)j} - \overline{a}_{(l)j})}{\sqrt{\sum_j (a_{(k)j} - \overline{a}_{(k)j})^2 \times \sum_j (a_{(l)j} - \overline{a}_{(l)j})^2}}\]

Meaning of Row Means (\(\overline{a}_{(k)j}\))

  • What does \(\overline{a}_{(k)j}\) represent?
    • It is simply the average of the row elements for node \(k\).
    • \[\overline{a}_{(k)j} = \frac{\sum_j a_{kj}}{N}\]
    • Where \(N\) is the number of columns (the order of the graph).
  • For a binary adjacency matrix, the row mean is simply the node degree divided by the number of nodes:
    • Node \(A\) has ties to \(\{C, D\}\) in a 6-node network:
    • Vector \(a_{(A)j} = (0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0)\).
    • Average: \(\overline{a}_{(A)j} = \frac{2}{6} \approx 0.33\).

Mean Differences Vector

  • Subtracting the row mean \(\overline{a}_{(A)j} \approx 0.33\) from each entry of \(A\)’s row vector \(a_{(A)j} = (0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0)\) gives the Mean Differences vector:

Iterated Correlation Distances: CONCOR

  • What happens if we take the correlation distance of a correlation distance matrix?
  • CONCOR (Convergence of Iterated Correlations; Breiger, Boorman, & Arabie, 1975):
    • If we recursively calculate the correlation distance matrix of the previous correlation matrix, the values mathematically converge to only \(+1.0\) and \(-1.0\).
    • This slices the network into two distinct, highly clear-cut blocks of structurally equivalent or similar nodes.
  • Let’s look at this mathematical convergence in action.

Part 2: Blockmodeling a 22-Node Network

Uncovering Secret Patterns in Large Networks

  • Let’s apply CONCOR to a larger, messier 22-node network on the right.
  • It contains:
    • Three high-degree hub nodes (\(A\), \(B\), \(C\)).
    • Sets of identical peripheral leaf nodes connected to each hub.
    • A highly central node \(D\) bridging hubs.
    • Two peripheral nodes \(U, V\) connected only to \(S\).
  • Let’s see how CONCOR recursively partitions this graph.

Step 1: First Split (Two Giant Blocks)

  • Running CONCOR on the whole 22-node network splits it into two massive blocks:
    • Block 1: \(\{V, U, S, H, G, F, E, T, C, A, B\}\) (Colored Red)
    • Block 2: \(\{R, Q, P, O, N, M, L, K, J, D, I\}\) (Colored Blue)

Step 2: Going Deeper (The Hierarchical Partition)

  • Running CONCOR recursively on the induced subgraphs of each block yields nested subdivisions:
    • Block 1 Splits:
      • Block 1a (Core hub/bridge): \(\{A, B, C, S, U, V\}\)
      • Block 1b (A’s peripheral leaves): \(\{E, F, G, H, T\}\)
    • Block 2 Splits:
      • Block 2a (B’s peripheral leaves): \(\{I, J, K, L, M\}\)
      • Block 2b (C’s peripheral leaves): \(\{N, O, P, Q, R\}\)
      • Block 2c (Unique central bridge): \(\{D\}\) (Occupies a block entirely by itself!)
  • Further splits on Block 1a isolate \(\{U, V\}\), and divide the remaining \(\{A, B, C, S\}\) into \(\{A, S\}\) and \(\{B, C\}\).

Part 3: Image Matrices & Reduced Graphs

Reducing Complexity to an Image Matrix

  • The blocked adjacency matrix can be simplified to a reduced image matrix (\(\mathbf{B}\)) of dimensions \(b \times b\) (where \(b = 7\) is the number of blocks).
  • Rule of Reduction:
    • We record a 0 if the relation block is a Zero Block (completely disconnected).
    • We record a 1 if the relation block is a One Block (contains ties).
  • This yields a highly simplified map of role interactions:
    • \[ \mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} \\ \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} \\ \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} \\ \text{One} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} \\ \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{One} \\ \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} \\ \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} & \text{One} & \text{One} & \text{Zero} & \text{Zero} \end{pmatrix} \]

Blockmodeling: A Structural Summary

  • Blockmodeling Distills Structure:
    • Slices complex social structures down to high-level system blueprints.
  • CONCOR algorithm:
    • Recursively polarizes Pearson correlations to group similar actors.
  • Zero vs. One Blocks:
    • Blocked adjacency matrices cleanly isolate patterns of connection (One blocks) and absolute separation (Zero blocks).
  • Reduced Graph Models:
    • Image matrices represent relations between roles, creating simplified reduced graphs that make networks easy to read, interpret, and model.
