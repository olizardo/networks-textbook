Graph Theory: Paths & Connectivity

The Basics of Social Network Analysis

Part 1: Indirect Connections & Paths

Adjacency vs. Indirect Connections

  • Adjacency captures direct connections between nodes:
    • If \(A\) is adjacent to \(B\), they share an edge.
  • But many network processes operate indirectly:
    • We must look beyond immediate contacts to the “friends of our friends.”
    • Diffusion Channels: Indirect connections act as channels for gossip, information transmission, disease contagion, or social influence.
  • In graph theory, indirect connections are formalized using three core concepts:
    • Path, Connectivity, and Reachability.

What is a Path?

  • A path between \(u\) and \(v\) is an alternating sequence of nodes and edges that:
    • Begins with an origin node (\(u\)).
    • Ends with a destination node (\(v\)).
    • Does not repeat any nodes (which implies edges are not repeated either!).
  • Anatomy of a Path (e.g. Red path from \(B\) to \(D\) on the right):
    • Written as: \(B-D = \{BE, ED\}\) (length \(l = 2\)).
    • Origin: \(B\), Destination: \(D\) (together called the end nodes).
    • Inner Nodes: \(E\) (the nodes in between).

Multiple Paths & Geodesics

  • Multiple distinct paths can connect the same pair of nodes.
  • Let’s trace paths from \(B\) to \(A\):
    • \(P_1 = \{BE, ED, DA\} \quad (l = 3)\)
    • \(P_2 = \{BE, EC, CA\} \quad (l = 3)\)
    • \(P_3 = \{BE, ED, DC, CA\} \quad (l = 4)\)
    • \(P_4 = \{BE, EC, CD, DA\} \quad (l = 4)\)
  • Shortest Path (Geodesic):
    • The path of minimum length between two nodes.
    • Geodesic Distance: The length of the geodesic path (here, geodesic distance \(d(B, A) = 3\), with tied geodesics \(P_1\) and \(P_2\)).

Multiple Shortest Paths (Geodesic Ties)

  • In real networks, geodesic distances are often tied:
    • There can be multiple distinct shortest paths of equal minimum length connecting the exact same pair of nodes.
  • Example: Shortest paths from \(B\) to \(A\) in our undirected graph:
    • Both paths have a geodesic distance of \(d(B, A) = 3\).
    • Geodesic Path 1: \(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A\) (Top)
    • Let’s visualize this path highlighted in red on the right.

Geodesic Ties: Redundancy & Reliability

  • Let’s look at the second geodesic path from \(B\) to \(A\):
    • Geodesic Path 2: \(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow C \rightarrow A\) (Bottom)
    • Let’s visualize this path highlighted in red on the right.
  • Redundancy and Reliability:
    • Having multiple geodesics of equal minimum length provides alternative routes of maximum efficiency.
    • Structural Robustness: If a node or edge on one path fails (e.g. node \(D\)), communication is completely unaffected because Path 2 remains open!

Sociological Significance of Geodesics

  • Why do social scientists care so much about shortest paths (geodesics) in real networks?
  • 1. Speed and Bandwidth of Transmission:
    • Information, trust, and influence decay as they travel along long paths.
    • Geodesic paths represent the channels through which diffusion (of gossip, innovation, or virus) happens fastest and with least loss.
  • 2. Power, Brokerage, and Betweenness:
    • Actors who lie on the geodesics of many other pairs of nodes in the network play a major role as information brokers or gatekeepers.
    • They control information flow, filter messages, and can extract rents or gain social influence (this is the basis of Betweenness Centrality).
  • 3. Search and the “Small World” Phenomenon:
    • Stanley Milgram’s classic “six degrees of separation” experiment showed that human social networks have surprisingly short geodesic distances.
    • Even in massive networks of millions of people, any two individuals are connected by incredibly short paths of friendship!

Node Eccentricity

  • Definition: The eccentricity of a node is the maximum geodesic distance from that node to any other node in the network.
    • It measures the “worst-case” distance to reach any other actor.
  • Example 1: Node E (Central Hub):
    • Distances from \(E\): \(d(E, B) = 1\), \(d(E, C) = 1\), \(d(E, D) = 1\), \(d(E, A) = 2\).
    • The maximum distance is \(2\) (to node \(A\)).
    • Eccentricity of \(E\) = 2.
  • Example 2: Node B (Peripheral Leaf):
    • Distances from \(B\): \(d(B, E) = 1\), \(d(B, C) = 2\), \(d(B, D) = 2\), \(d(B, A) = 3\).
    • The maximum distance is \(3\) (to node \(A\)).
    • Eccentricity of \(B\) = 3.

What is a Cycle?

  • A cycle is a path of length 3 or larger that starts and ends on the same node.
  • Key Concepts:
    • Origin and destination are identical (the same vertex).
    • All inner nodes and all edges must be completely unique.
    • Cycles are the fundamental building blocks of network redundancy and feedback loops.

Visualizing a Simple Cycle

  • Let’s look at a simple cycle highlighted in our graph:
    • Cycle: \(C \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow C\).
    • It has a length of 3 (consisting of 3 edges).
  • Verification of Properties:
    • Origin and destination are identical (both are node \(C\)).
    • All intermediate nodes (\(D\), \(A\)) are unique.
    • All traversed edges are unique.
    • This closed loop creates a fundamental feedback pathway.

Graph Girth

  • Definition: The girth of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle.
  • Properties:
    • If a graph contains no cycles at all (like a tree graph), its girth is defined as infinity (\(\infty\)).
    • For our 5-node undirected graph on the right, the shortest cycle is the triangle: \[E \rightarrow D \rightarrow C \rightarrow E\]
    • Thus, the girth of this graph is 3.

Graph Circumference

  • Definition: The circumference of a graph is the length of its longest cycle.
  • Properties:
    • If a graph is acyclic, its circumference is defined as zero or undefined.
    • For our 5-node undirected graph on the right, the longest cycle is the four-loop: \[E \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow C \rightarrow E\]
    • Thus, the circumference of this graph is 4.

Special Paths: Hamiltonian Paths

  • Hamiltonian Path: A path that visits every single node in the graph exactly once.
  • Our Example Graph: Shown on the right, it has 5 nodes \(\{A, B, C, D, E\}\).
  • A Hamiltonian Path: Let’s trace one in red: \[A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E\]
    • Every node is visited exactly once.
    • The diagonal edge \(B-E\) remains untraversed.

Special Paths: Hamiltonian Cycles

  • Hamiltonian Cycle: A cycle that visits every single node in the graph exactly once and returns to the origin.
  • Our Hamiltonian Cycle: Let’s trace it in red on the right: \[A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E \rightarrow A\]
    • This cycle forms the complete outer pentagonal ring.
    • Visits all 5 nodes exactly once and closes the loop!
    • Degree Condition: Since every node has degree \(\ge 2\), a Hamiltonian cycle is successfully enabled.

Walks, Trails, and Paths

  • Walk: Any sequence of alternating nodes and edges connecting \(u\) to \(v\).
    • No restrictions: Nodes and edges can be repeated as much as you want!
    • e.g., \(\{B \rightarrow E \rightarrow C \rightarrow E \rightarrow D\}\) (traverses \(E\) and edge \(E-C\) twice).
  • Trail: A walk that does not repeat any edges.
    • Repeated nodes are allowed, but every edge in the sequence must be unique.
    • e.g., \(\{A \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E \rightarrow C \rightarrow B\}\) (repeats node \(C\) but all edges are unique).
  • Path: A walk that does not repeat any nodes (which means edges cannot be repeated either).
  • The Nested Hierarchy: All paths are trails, and all trails are walks, but the reverse is not true: \[Paths \subset Trails \subset Walks\]

Walks: A Visual Example

  • Walk (B-E-C-E-D):
    • We start at node \(B\), walk to \(E\), then \(C\), then back to \(E\), and finally to \(D\).
    • Repeated Node: Node \(E\) is visited twice in this sequence.
    • Repeated Edge: Edge \(E-C\) (and \(C-E\)) is traversed twice.
    • Highlighted in red on the right.

Trails: A Visual Example

  • Trail (B-E-C-D-E-A):
    • We start at \(B \rightarrow E \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E \rightarrow A\).
    • Repeated Node: Node \(E\) is visited twice.
    • Repeated Edges: None! Every edge in the sequence is completely unique.
    • Highlighted in red on the right.

Paths in Directed Graphs (Digraphs)

  • In directed graphs, paths must strictly respect the direction of the arrows!
  • Let’s trace directed paths from \(A\) to \(B\) in our digraph:
    • \(P_1 = \{AC, CE, EB\} \quad (l = 3)\)
    • \(P_2 = \{AD, DE, EB\} \quad (l = 3)\)
    • \(P_3 = \{AC, CD, DE, EB\} \quad (l = 4)\)
  • But can we find any directed path from \(B\) to \(A\)?
    • No! All arrows lead away from \(A\) and toward \(B\).

Directed Reachability

  • Asymmetric Reachability:
    • Node \(A\) can reach \(B\) via directed path \(\{AD, DE, EB\}\) (\(l_{AB} = 3\)).
    • But node \(B\) cannot reach \(A\) because we cannot travel against the arrows (\(l_{BA} = \infty\)).
  • Mutual Reachability:
    • Two nodes \(u\) and \(v\) are mutually reachable if there exists a directed path from \(u \rightarrow v\) AND from \(v \rightarrow u\).
    • These two paths do not have to be the same length!
    • Example: Nodes \(B\) and \(C\) are mutually reachable:
      • \(B\) reaches \(C\) via \(\{BE, ED, DC\} \quad (l = 3)\)
      • \(C\) reaches \(B\) via shortest path \(\{CE, EB\} \quad (l = 2)\)

Directed Reachability: Visual Examples

Directed Cycles & DAGs

  • A directed cycle is a cycle where all arrows flow in a single, continuous direction.
    • Example: Cycle starting and ending at \(C\):
      • \(\{CE, ED, DC\} \quad (l = 3)\)
  • Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs):
    • Directed graphs that contain no directed cycles.
    • e.g. strict organizational command structures, directed trees.
    • Anti-symmetric relations (like “is boss of”) structurally forbid cycles to maintain consistent hierarchy.

Ignoring the Arrows: Semipaths

  • What if we want to trace connections in a directed graph but ignore the arrows?
  • This is called a semipath (or semicycle if it is a closed loop).
  • A semipath treats directed arcs as undirected edges.
  • Example: Semipath connecting \(B\) and \(A\):
    • \(\{BE, EC, CA\}\) (highlighted in red on the right).

Part 4: Edge and Node Connectivity (Cutsets & Bridges)

Edge Cutsets & Connectivity

  • We can disconnect a connected graph by removing a subset of its edges.
  • Edge Cutset:
    • Any set of edges whose removal disconnects the graph into two or more components.
  • Minimum Edge Cutset:
    • An edge cutset containing the minimum possible number of edges needed to disconnect the graph.
  • Edge-Connectivity (\(\lambda(G)\)):
    • The number of edges in a minimum edge cutset.
  • Bridge:
    • A single edge whose removal disconnects the graph (a minimum edge cutset of size 1, so \(\lambda(G) = 1\)).

Edge Cutsets: A Visual Example

  • In our 5-node graph on the right:
    • Edges A-C and B-C are highlighted in red (Before).
  • Removing both edges:
    • Disconnects \(\{C, D, E\}\) from \(\{A, B\}\) (After).
    • This is an edge cutset of size 2.

Bridges: A Cutset of Size 1

  • Bridge:
    • A single edge whose removal disconnects the graph (so \(\lambda(G) = 1\)).
  • In our graph on the right, the edge C-D is highlighted in red.
    • Removing edge C-D completely disconnects \(\{A, B, C\}\) from \(\{D, E\}\).
    • Bridges represent the most critical, yet vulnerable transmission links in any network.

Special Properties of Bridges

  • In a connected graph, a bridge (such as the red C-D edge on the right) possesses two interesting mathematical properties (Buckley and Harary 1990):
  • 1. The No-Cycle Rule:
    • A bridge never lies on any cycle of the graph.
    • If it did, removing it would still leave the rest of the cycle intact, failing to disconnect the graph.
  • 2. The Intermediation Bottleneck:
    • The bridge is an edge on every path connecting at least one pair of nodes (e.g., any node in \(\{A, B, C\}\) to any node in \(\{D, E\}\)).
    • Every packet of information flowing between these clusters must pass through this single bottleneck.

Local Bridges

  • In a classic paper on “The Strength of Weak Ties” (1973), Mark Granovetter developed the concept of a local bridge:
    • An edge that, if removed, does not disconnect the entire graph, but significantly increases the shortest path length between its end nodes.
  • Sociological Power:
    • Local bridges act as crucial “shortcuts” that facilitate efficient, cross-group communication.
    • They are the primary channels through which novel information diffuses between separate social clusters.

Local Bridge: A Visual Example

  • Let’s look at the edge C-D highlighted in red:
    • It connects the left cluster \(\{A, B, C\}\) to the right cluster \(\{D, E, F\}\).
  • Is it a bridge? No. If removed, the graph remains connected via the bottom path (\(B-G-H-I-E\)).
  • Is it a local bridge? Yes!
    • Observed distance: \(d(C, D) = 1\).
    • If removed, the distance increases to: \[d(C, D) = 5 \quad (C-B-G-H-I-E-D)\]

Node Cutsets & Connectivity

  • We can also disconnect a graph by removing nodes (vertices).
  • Removing a node also removes all edges incident to it—nodes “take their links with them”!
  • Node Cutset (Vertex Cutset):
    • Any set of nodes whose removal disconnects the remaining graph into two or more components.
  • Minimum Node Cutset:
    • A node cutset containing the minimum possible number of nodes needed to disconnect the graph.
  • Node-Connectivity (\(\kappa(G)\)):
    • The number of nodes in a minimum node cutset.
  • Articulation Node (Cutpoint):
    • A single node whose removal disconnects the graph (so \(\kappa(G) = 1\)).

Articulation Nodes: A Visual Example

  • In our house graph on the right, node E is highlighted in red.
  • Removing node E:
    • Removes all of its incident edges: \(B-E, E-C, E-D\).
    • Completely disconnects node \(B\) from the rest of the network.
    • This is a node cutset of size 1 (Node \(E\) is an articulation node).

Special Properties of Articulation Nodes

  • An articulation node (or cutpoint, such as E on the right) has unique properties mirroring those of bridges:
  • 1. Node Equivalent of a Bridge:
    • A single actor upon whom the entire connectivity of the network depends (\(\kappa(G) = 1\)).
  • 2. The Path Intermediator:
    • It stands as an inner node on every single path connecting some node \(v\) to the rest of the network (e.g., node \(E\) is on every path connecting \(B\) to \(\{A, C, D\}\)).
  • 3. The End-Node Rule:
    • A node of degree \(1\) (an end-node, like \(B\)) can never be an articulation node; removing it always leaves the remaining graph connected.

Node Cutset: An Edge Cutset Comparison

  • Let’s look at node \(A\) in our house graph on the right.
  • To disconnect \(A\) by removing edges, we must remove 2 edges: \(C-A\) and \(D-A\) (red).
  • But to disconnect \(A\) by removing nodes, we only need to remove 1 node: \(E\) (the cutpoint that isolates \(B\)).
  • This illustrates how node and edge connectivity can differ in the same graph.

k-Connected Graphs & Robustness

  • \(k\)-Connected Graph:
    • A graph is \(k\)-connected (or \(k\)-node connected) if the minimum number of nodes required to disconnect it is \(k\).
    • 1-Connected: Removing 1 node disconnects the graph (contains an articulation point).
    • 2-Connected: Requires removing at least 2 nodes to disconnect it.
    • 3-Connected: Requires removing at least 3 nodes to disconnect it.
  • Menger’s Theorem (1927):
    • If a graph is \(k\)-connected, there are at least \(k\) node-independent paths (paths that do not share any common inner nodes) connecting each non-adjacent pair of vertices.
  • Social Cohesion: Higher \(k\)-connectivity indicates a more robust, cohesive, and redundant communication structure.

    • A graph where it requires removing at least 2 nodes to disconnect it.
  • Before (Left):
    • A 6-node cycle with a diagonal chord (edge 2-5), with nodes 2 and 5 highlighted in red.

3-Connectivity: A Visual Example

    • A graph where it requires removing at least 3 nodes to disconnect it.
  • Before (Left):

  • After (Right):

Graph Cutsets: Quantitative Cohesion

  • Cohesion can be directly translated to graph-theoretic cutsets:
    • Cohesive Groups: Have large edge-cutsets and node-cutsets. Many connections or individuals must be removed to disrupt or fragment the group.
    • Fragmented Groups: Have small cutsets (such as bridges or single cutpoints). They are vulnerable to rapid disruption if key facilitators are removed.
  • Any network can be decomposed into hierarchical subgraphs with specific levels of \(k\)-connectivity.

The Connectivity Inequality

  • For any undirected graph \(G\), node-connectivity (\(\kappa\)), edge-connectivity (\(\lambda\)), and minimum degree (\(\delta\)) obey a fundamental structural hierarchy: \[\kappa(G) \le \lambda(G) \le \delta(G)\]
  • A Strict Inequality Example (\(1 < 2 < 3\)):
    • In our 9-node “double-clique” graph on the right:
      • Minimum Degree (\(\delta = 3\)): The least connected nodes (\(3, 4, 7, 8\)) have exactly 3 edges.
      • Edge-Connectivity (\(\lambda = 2\)): Removing \(\{1-9, 2-9\}\) separates the left clique from the waist. No single bridge exists.
      • Node-Connectivity (\(\kappa = 1\)): Removing the central cutpoint (node 9) completely disconnects the graph.

Part 5: Directed Connectivity & Components

Flavors of Connectivity in Directed Graphs

  • Because of edge directionality, directed graphs have three major levels of connectivity:
  1. Strongly Connected: Every pair of nodes is mutually reachable via directed paths.
    • Recursively Connected (Subtype): Strongly connected, AND the directed paths in both directions share the exact same set of intermediate nodes (sharing both vertices and edges).
  2. Unilaterally Connected: Every pair is connected by at least one directed path (from \(u \rightarrow v\) OR \(v \rightarrow u\), but not necessarily both).
  3. Weakly Connected: Every pair of nodes is connected by at least a semipath (connected if we ignore arrow directions).

Strongly Connected: A Visual Example

  • Strong Connectivity:
    • A directed graph is strongly connected if there is a directed path from every node to every other node (mutual reachability).
  • 7-Node Example (Right):
    • Consists of a complete spanning cycle (\(A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D \rightarrow E \rightarrow F \rightarrow G \rightarrow A\)) plus chord arcs.
    • Because of the continuous cycle, any node can reach any other node, ensuring complete mutual reachability.

Unilaterally Connected: A Visual Example

  • Unilateral Connectivity:
    • A directed graph is unilaterally connected if for every pair of nodes, there is at least one directed path connecting them.
  • 7-Node Example (Right):
    • Same 7 nodes, but the feedback edge \(G \rightarrow A\) and other backward chords are removed. Edges flow strictly forward (\(A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \dots \rightarrow G\)).
    • Here, \(A\) can reach \(G\), but \(G\) cannot reach \(A\) (no mutual reachability).
    • However, for every single pair, at least one directed pathway exists.

Weakly Connected: A Visual Example

  • Weak Connectivity:
    • A directed graph that is connected only if we ignore the direction of the arrowheads (treating it like an undirected graph).
  • 7-Node Example (Right):
    • Uses our textbook’s 7-node directed graph.
    • Nodes \(E\) and \(G\) are transmitter nodes (indegree 0) that cannot reach each other, nor can they be reached by anyone else.
    • Thus, \(\{E, G\}\) is a mutually unreachable pair (violating unilateral connectivity).

Decomposing Directed Components

  • Like undirected graphs, directed graphs can be decomposed into:
  • Weakly Connected Components (WCCs):
    • Subgraphs connected when ignoring edge directions.
    • Yields one WCC containing all 7 nodes: \(\{A, B, C, D, E, F, G\}\).
  • Strongly Connected Components (SCCs):
    • Subgraphs where every pair is mutually reachable.
    • Yields four SCCs:
      • \(\{A, B, D, F\}\) (the core “rumor mill”, highlighted in red).
      • Singletons: \(\{C\}\), \(\{E\}\), \(\{G\}\).

Part 6: Tree Graphs & Forests

What is a Tree Graph?

  • If a graph is both connected and has no cycles (acyclic), then it is a tree graph.
  • The Cycle Restriction:
    • A cycle is a path that begins and ends with the same node.
    • In a tree graph, you can never start with a node and trace a sequence of distinct edges that take you back to where you started!

Properties of Tree Graphs

  • Tree graphs possess several unique mathematical and structural properties:
    1. The Single Path Property:
      • Every pair of nodes in a tree graph is linked by exactly one unique path.
      • There are no redundant or alternative paths between any two actors.
    2. The One-Cycle Property:
      • Adding a single new edge connecting any pair of non-adjacent nodes always creates exactly one cycle, destroying its tree status.

Tree Graph Metrics: Vertices & Edges

  • In any tree graph, the number of edges (\(m\)) is always exactly equal to the number of nodes (\(n\)) minus one: \[m = n - 1 \qquad \iff \qquad n = m + 1\]
  • Sum of Degrees Identity:
    • Recall that the sum of degrees in any undirected graph is \(2m\).
    • Substituting \(m = n - 1\), the sum of degrees of a tree graph is always: \[\sum_i k_i = 2(n - 1) = 2n - 2\]
    • In a tree graph, the sum of degrees is always exactly twice the number of nodes minus two!

Tree Graph Density

  • Because of the strict \(m = n - 1\) edge constraint, tree graph density simplifies beautifully.
  • Density of a Tree Graph: \[d(T) = \frac{2m}{n(n-1)} = \frac{2(n-1)}{n(n-1)} = \frac{2}{n}\]
  • For our 5-node tree graph on the right (\(n = 5\)):
    • The density is: \[d(T) = \frac{2}{5} = 0.40 \quad (40\%)\]

Tree Graph Average Degree

  • Average degree in a tree graph also simplifies beautifully due to the edge constraint.
  • Average Degree of a Tree Graph: \[\bar{k} = \frac{\sum k_i}{n} = \frac{2m}{n} = \frac{2n - 2}{n}\]
  • For our 5-node tree graph on the right (\(n = 5\)):
    • The average degree is: \[\bar{k} = \frac{2(5) - 2}{5} = \frac{8}{5} = 1.6\]

Trees as Minimally Connected Graphs

  • Trees are minimally connected graphs:
    • They are connected using the smallest possible number of edges needed to connect \(n\) vertices.
  • The Bridges Rule:
    • Removing even a single edge from a tree graph completely disconnects the graph.
    • Thus, every edge of a tree graph is a bridge (a minimum edge cutset of size 1)!
  • Disconnecting a tree graph by removing any edge splits it into separate connected components, which are themselves trees.

Numerical Example: 8-Node Tree

Let’s compute the metrics for the 8-node tree shown on the right:

  • 1. Nodes and Edges:
    • Number of nodes: \(n = 8\)
    • Number of edges: \(m = n - 1 = 7\)
  • 2. Degree of each node:
    • \(k_A = 2, k_B = 3, k_C = 3, k_D = 1, k_E = 2, k_F = 1, k_G = 1, k_H = 1\)
    • Sum of degrees: \(\sum k_i = 14\)
    • Check: \(2(n-1) = 2(7) = 14 \quad \checkmark\)
  • 3. Network Density:
    • \(d(T) = \frac{2}{n} = \frac{2}{8} = 0.25 \quad (25\%)\)
  • 4. Average Degree:
    • \(\bar{k} = \frac{2n - 2}{n} = \frac{14}{8} = 1.75\)

Special Types of Trees: Line, Star & Caterpillar Graphs

  • The Line Graph (\(L_n\)):
    • A single straight path connecting \(n\) nodes.
    • Used to model sequential diffusion processes (like the “Telephone Game”).
  • The Star Graph (\(S_n\)):
    • A central hub connected to \(n-1\) leaf nodes.
    • Represents extreme structural centralization.
  • The Caterpillar Graph:
    • A central path (“body”) with pendant leaf nodes (“legs”) sticking out of it.

The Graph Efficiency

  • Concept (Krackhardt, 1994):
    • Measures how economical the connectivity of a graph is.
    • A graph is efficiently connected if it uses the minimum possible number of edges to remain connected.
  • Formula: \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(m - n + 1)}{(n-1)(n-2)}\right]\]
    • Where \(m\) is the number of edges and \(n\) is the number of nodes.
  • Numerical Proof (The Tree on the Right):
    • Pictured on the right is a tree of \(n = 5\) nodes and \(m = 4\) edges.
    • Substituting \(n = 5\) and \(m = 4\) into the formula: \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(4 - 5 + 1)}{(5-1)(5-2)}\right]\] \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(0)}{4 \times 3}\right] = 1 - 0 = 1.0\]
  • Beyond Trees (Decreasing Efficiency):
    • As edges are added beyond \(m = n - 1\), the numerator \((m - n + 1) > 0\), causing \(F(G)\) to decrease toward \(0.0\).

Detailed Example: Graph 1 (Less Efficient)

  • Graph 1 Structure:
    • Contains \(n = 5\) nodes and \(m = 7\) edges.
  • Efficiency Calculation:
    • We substitute \(n=5\) and \(m=7\) into the formula: \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(7 - 5 + 1)}{(5-1)(5-2)}\right]\] \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(3)}{4 \times 3}\right] = 1 - \frac{6}{12} = 0.50\]
  • Interpretation:
    • With 5 nodes, a connected tree needs only 4 edges.
    • This graph has 3 redundant edges (\(7 - 5 + 1 = 3\)), resulting in a low efficiency score of \(0.50\).

Detailed Example: Graph 2 (More Efficient)

  • Graph 2 Structure:
    • Contains \(n = 7\) nodes and \(m = 10\) edges.
  • Efficiency Calculation:
    • We substitute \(n=7\) and \(m=10\) into the formula: \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(10 - 7 + 1)}{(7-1)(7-2)}\right]\] \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(4)}{6 \times 5}\right] = 1 - \frac{8}{30} \approx 0.73\]
  • Conclusion:
    • Despite having more edges (\(10\) vs \(7\)), Graph 2 is larger. Its redundancy is distributed over more nodes, yielding a higher efficiency of \(0.73\).
    • Graph 2 is more efficient than Graph 1 (\(0.73 > 0.50\)).

Graph Efficiency: Complete Graphs

  • Maximally Inefficient:
    • The complete graph (\(K_n\)) is minimally efficient because it has the maximum possible number of edges.
    • Number of edges: \(m = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\)
  • Example (\(n=5\) Complete Graph):
    • Edges: \(m = \frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10\)
    • Calculation: \[F(G) = 1 - \left[\frac{2(10 - 5 + 1)}{(5-1)(5-2)}\right]\] \[= 1 - \left[\frac{2(6)}{4 \times 3}\right] = 1 - \frac{12}{12} = 0.0\]
    • Thus, any complete graph yields \(F(G) = 0.0\).
